### Federal Jury Verdict — Pregnancy Discrimination, Title VII and NYSHRL

**Unanimous Liability Verdict + Prevailing-Party Attorneys' Fees** — May 2017

**Weng v. Fancy Lee Sushi Bar and Grill, Inc., et al.**
Case No. 15-cv-5737 (ADS) (E.D.N.Y.)

Steven tried this federal pregnancy discrimination case to verdict alongside co-counsel in the Eastern District of New York, before the Honorable Arthur D. Spatt. The plaintiff was a long-time waitress and bartender at a Long Island restaurant who was pulled off the schedule and ultimately fired within days of telling her manager she was pregnant. The defense argued at trial that the termination was for the plaintiff's own benefit — to protect her and the pregnancy — and not discriminatory.

After deliberation, the jury unanimously rejected that defense. The jurors found the restaurant liable for pregnancy discrimination under Title VII and the New York State Human Rights Law, and separately found that the supervisor who fired the plaintiff had personally participated in the discriminatory conduct, imposing individual liability on her as well. Following the verdict, the trial team successfully pursued and obtained an award of attorneys' fees as the prevailing party.

Pregnancy discrimination cases are notoriously difficult to win in front of a jury. The defense almost always involves some version of a "concerned employer" narrative — that the employer was looking out for the worker's health rather than punishing her for being pregnant — and jurors can be receptive to that framing. Securing a unanimous federal verdict against both the corporate employer and the individual supervisor is a meaningful result for trial work in this area of the law.

### Motion to Dismiss Denied — Adult Survivor Act, Revived Sexual Harassment Claims

**Claims Sustained Under New York's Adult Survivor Act** — November 2023

**Roudabush v. Albert Einstein College of Medicine of Yeshiva University**
Index No. 807857/2023E (Bronx County Supreme Court)

Steven defeated a motion to dismiss in a closely contested application of New York's Adult Survivor Act (CPLR 214-j) — the statute that revives otherwise time-barred civil claims arising from conduct that would constitute a sexual offense under Penal Law article 130. The defense argued that the alleged conduct — a professor who repeatedly tugged the plaintiff's ponytail during class and later poked her bare stomach in front of other students — did not rise to a "sexual offense" and therefore could not be revived under the ASA.

The court disagreed. Affording the plaintiff every favorable inference, the court held that the allegations could plausibly constitute "forcible touching" of intimate parts under Penal Law § 130.52, and that the bare stomach qualified as an "intimate part" within the meaning of the statute. The plaintiff's New York City Human Rights Law claims for gender discrimination, hostile work environment, retaliation, and vicarious liability all survived and proceeded to discovery — a meaningful result on a motion the defense was confident it would win.

### Summary Judgment Denied — Age Discrimination

**Defendants' Motion Denied in Full** — June 2023

**Kamal v. G E R Industries, Inc., et al.**
Case No. 7:20-cv-05671-VB-VR (S.D.N.Y.)

Steven represented an older worker who believed he had been mistreated and pushed out of his job because of his age, and litigated the case in the Southern District of New York against both the corporate employer and the individually named owner of the business.

Age discrimination cases are among the most difficult employment matters to prosecute. Employers rarely admit that age played any role, the federal age discrimination statute requires a plaintiff to prove that age was the "but-for" cause of the adverse action, and defendants almost always come into court with a list of facially legitimate, non-age reasons for the decision — performance concerns, restructuring, business needs — that the plaintiff then has to discredit. The difficulty is reflected in how often age cases are dismissed at the summary judgment stage, before a jury ever hears the worker's side of the story.

After full briefing, the court denied the defendants' motion for summary judgment in its entirety, clearing the way for trial against both the corporate defendant and the individual owner — and giving Steven's client his day in court on the age discrimination claim.

### Summary Judgment Substantially Denied — Retaliation Sustained Despite Client's Written Resignation

**Retaliation, Failure-to-Promote, Aiding-and-Abetting, and Assault & Battery Claims Sustained** — March 2024

**Ferrando-Dehtiar v. Anesthesia Group of Albany, P.C., et al.**
Case No. 1:20-cv-01373 (AMN/CFH) (N.D.N.Y.)

Steven secured a substantial denial of summary judgment in a federal Title VII and New York State Human Rights Law action against an anesthesiology practice and three of its physician-partners. The hardest fight was retaliation. The firm's client had submitted a written notice of resignation shortly after complaining of gender discrimination and harassment, then several weeks later attempted to rescind that resignation. The employer refused and held her to the original separation date.

On those facts, most courts would have treated the resignation as voluntary and dismissed the retaliation claim. Steven persuaded the court otherwise — arguing that the refusal to allow rescission was itself the adverse employment action, and that a reasonable jury could find it was caused by retaliatory animus rather than the employer's proffered legitimate reasons. The court adopted the Fifth Circuit's reasoning in *Porter v. Houma Terrebonne Housing Authority Board of Commissioners* and held that, on this record, the denial of rescission could constitute retaliation. The retaliation claim survived against the corporate defendant and each of the three individual physician-partners.

The court also denied summary judgment on the failure-to-promote claims under Title VII and the NYSHRL against the corporate defendant and two of the individual partners, on the NYSHRL aiding-and-abetting discrimination claims against all three individual defendants, and on the New York common-law assault and battery claims against the harassing partner.

### Summary Judgment Denied — Pregnancy Discrimination, Falsified Resignation During Maternity Leave

**Defendants' Motion Denied; Case Resolved by Settlement** — December 2022

**Crowley v. Vintage Home Entertainment, Inc., et al.**
Index No. 526211/2019 (Kings County Supreme Court)

Steven represented a sales director who was on approved New York Paid Family Leave following the birth of her child when her PFL administrator called her to report that her employer had told the state insurance carrier she had resigned. She had not resigned. Her PFL paperwork — signed by the company's own president — set her return-to-work date for the following month, and she was actively receiving leave payments.

When the plaintiff confronted the company, the financial controller responded in writing that "as per [the president], you resigned," and then pressed her to send in a backdated letter of resignation to paper over the issue. She refused. After that refusal, the company changed course and offered to bring her back to work. By that point — having watched her employer lie about her status to a state agency in the middle of her maternity leave — the plaintiff declined the offer.

On summary judgment, the defense framed the entire episode as an innocent misunderstanding that the company had cured by inviting the plaintiff back, and argued that as a matter of law she had suffered no adverse employment action. This is a notoriously difficult fact pattern for a plaintiff: when an employer has put a return-to-work offer in writing, courts frequently accept the "misunderstanding" framing and dispose of the case at summary judgment.

The Kings County Supreme Court rejected that framing. Drawing every favorable inference for the plaintiff, the court held that the defendants had failed to offer any legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for advising the insurance carrier that she had resigned, and that triable issues of fact existed as to whether the disruption of her paid family leave was an attempt to terminate her employment under cover of a fabricated resignation — giving rise to an inference of pregnancy discrimination. Following the denial of the motion, the case resolved by settlement.

### Motion to Dismiss Denied — FLSA Wage Theory Preserves Federal Jurisdiction Over NYCHRL Discrimination Claims

**Defendants' Rule 12(b) Motion Denied in Full** — June 2023

**Pradhan v. Maleen Banquet Hall and Mani Singh, Individually**
Case No. 22-CV-3533 (AMD)(JMW) (E.D.N.Y.)

Steven defeated a defense motion to dismiss in a national-origin discrimination, hostile work environment, and assault matter — and, in doing so, preserved federal jurisdiction in a case where the entire jurisdictional hook turned on a single carefully-pled wage theory.

The plaintiff was a general manager at a Long Island banquet hall who endured repeated derogatory comments about his Nepali national origin and, on one occasion, a physical assault by the owner. The natural home for the discrimination claims was the New York City Human Rights Law — a notably more plaintiff-friendly statute than its federal counterpart. To bring those NYCHRL claims into federal court, which is a meaningfully tougher venue for defendants of this kind, Steven needed a federal-question hook.

The hook was unconventional. The plaintiff held a title — general manager — that would ordinarily place him within the FLSA's managerial exemption and out of reach of the federal wage statute entirely. But during the plaintiff's last two weeks of employment, the defendants failed to pay him at all, dropping his compensation below the salary-basis threshold required to keep the exemption intact. Steven pled the FLSA claim around that window and brought the NYCHRL discrimination, hostile work environment, and assault claims into federal court under supplemental jurisdiction.

The defendants moved to dismiss the FLSA claim — arguing the plaintiff's manager title made him exempt as a matter of law — and, with it, the state-law claims for want of supplemental jurisdiction. Judge Ann M. Donnelly denied the motion in full. The court held that the managerial exemption is an affirmative defense that is not apparent on the face of the complaint, that whether the plaintiff was actually paid on a salary basis was a fact-bound question requiring discovery, and — because the FLSA claim survived — federal jurisdiction over the NYCHRL claims was preserved.

Keeping NYCHRL claims in federal court after a Rule 12(b) challenge to the wage hook is a non-trivial result. Many courts will sever or decline supplemental jurisdiction over state-law discrimination claims when the federal wage claim looks fragile, on the theory that the two sets of claims lack a sufficiently close factual nexus. Here, the entire claim structure stayed intact in the Eastern District of New York.

### Discovery Order — Ex Parte Access to Defendant's Former Supervisor Preserved

**Defense's Attempt to Bar Communication with Key Witness Rejected** — March 2019

**Lozama v. Samaritan Daytop Village, Inc., et al.**
Case No. 18-CV-4351 (DLI)(RML) (E.D.N.Y.)

In a federal employment discrimination action, Steven won a closely-litigated discovery dispute over his right to communicate ex parte with a former supervisor at the defendant organization — a witness whose account was material to building the plaintiff's case.

After Steven had already interviewed the former supervisor without notice to or consent from defense counsel, the defense moved to bar any further contact. Their argument: under the New York Rules of Professional Conduct, even a former employee whose conduct could be imputed to the corporation for liability purposes qualifies as a "party" represented by the company's lawyers. If that reading had been accepted, it would have shut down one of the most important informal-discovery tools available to a plaintiff-side employment lawyer — direct access to ex-employees, who often hold the most candid view of what actually happened inside the workplace.

Steven fought the position hard. The United States Magistrate Judge agreed with him and rejected the defense's reading of the rule. The court's memorandum decision held that Rule 4.2 and its predecessor "do not apply in any context to a corporation's former employees," that the bright-line rule of *Niesig v. Team I* governs in New York, and that Steven could continue to communicate with the former supervisor without notice to defense counsel — subject only to the standard duty to steer clear of privileged or confidential information.

The decision preserved a critical witness for the plaintiff's case and turned back a defense strategy that, if it had succeeded, would have meaningfully narrowed plaintiffs' lawyers' access to former-employee witnesses across the Eastern District of New York.
