<h2 id="what-is-pregnancy-discrimination">What Is Pregnancy Discrimination at Work?</h2>

Pregnancy discrimination happens when an employer treats an employee worse because of pregnancy, childbirth, lactation, fertility treatment, pregnancy-related medical conditions, miscarriage, abortion, or plans to become pregnant. It can happen during hiring, scheduling, job assignments, promotions, discipline, leave, return-to-work decisions, layoffs, or termination.

Pregnancy discrimination is often disguised as concern. An employer may say it is trying to "protect" a pregnant employee, that the job is "too stressful," that clients will not understand, or that the employee should focus on family. Those statements may sound softer than direct hostility, but they can still reflect illegal stereotypes about pregnant workers.

Pregnancy discrimination can also involve powerful decision-makers. A CEO, owner, founder, partner, manager, or executive may assume that an employee will be less committed after becoming pregnant or having a child. The law does not allow employers to make employment decisions based on those assumptions.

<h2 id="fired-for-being-pregnant">Can I Be Fired for Being Pregnant in New York or New Jersey?</h2>

No. Federal law, the New York State Human Rights Law, the New York City Human Rights Law, and the New Jersey Law Against Discrimination all prohibit firing you because of pregnancy, childbirth, or a related medical condition. Pregnancy does not have to be the only reason. If it played a role, the firing may still be illegal. New York State and New Jersey law cover employers of any size. An employer can still fire a pregnant employee for a genuine reason unrelated to pregnancy.

Employers rarely admit pregnancy was the reason for a termination. Instead, the firing may be called "restructuring," buried in a round of "layoffs," or explained with reasons that keep shifting. A false explanation used to cover up discrimination is called pretext.

Figuring out whether a termination was based on pregnancy discrimination can leave an employee guessing what they did wrong. In our experience, when an employee believes pregnancy may have played a role in their termination, that instinct is usually worth trusting.

In our experience, employers often involve their lawyers behind the scenes early, sometimes months before a termination is carried out, and they rarely leave a paper trail. Many present a low-ball severance offer at termination, hoping you will sign it and waive your rights without speaking to a lawyer.

If you were fired while pregnant:

- Do not sign a severance agreement until an employment lawyer reviews it.
- Write down a timeline: when you disclosed your pregnancy and what changed afterward.
- Save performance reviews, emails, and texts you lawfully have access to.
- Act quickly. Some deadlines are short.

<h2 id="pregnancy-discrimination-laws">What Laws Protect Pregnant Employees in New York and New Jersey?</h2>

New York City employees are protected by the **New York City Human Rights Law**, including N.Y.C. Admin. Code § 8-107. The NYCHRL prohibits discrimination based on pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical conditions and is interpreted broadly in favor of protecting employees. New York employees may also be protected by the **New York State Human Rights Law**, N.Y. Exec. Law § 296, and federal law, including **Title VII** and the **Pregnancy Discrimination Act**.

Under federal law, the Pregnancy Discrimination Act amended Title VII to make clear that discrimination because of pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions is a form of sex discrimination. In *Young v. United Parcel Service, Inc.*, 575 U.S. 206 (2015), the U.S. Supreme Court addressed when an employer's refusal to accommodate pregnancy-related work restrictions may support a pregnancy discrimination claim. New York courts have also addressed pregnancy accommodation claims under the NYSHRL and NYCHRL, including *Coronado v. Weill Cornell Medical College*, 66 Misc. 3d 404 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 2019).

New Jersey employees are protected by the **New Jersey Law Against Discrimination**, N.J.S.A. 10:5-12, which prohibits pregnancy discrimination and requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations for pregnancy, childbirth, lactation, and related medical conditions, unless doing so would cause undue hardship.

The **Pregnant Workers Fairness Act**, 42 U.S.C. § 2000gg (the "PWFA"), is a federal law that took effect on June 27, 2023. It requires employers with 15 or more employees to provide reasonable accommodations for known limitations related to an employee's pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions, unless the accommodation would cause "undue hardship," meaning significant difficulty or expense. The condition does not have to be a disability. An employer cannot force you onto leave if another accommodation would let you keep working. Common accommodations include extra breaks, a stool or seating, help with lifting, and time off for prenatal appointments.

The PWFA sets a federal minimum. It does not replace New York State, New York City, or New Jersey law, which protect pregnant workers on their own. [Learn what the PWFA and New York law require](/blog/pregnancy-accommodations-under-the-pwfa).

<h2 id="examples">What Does Pregnancy Discrimination Look Like?</h2>

Pregnancy discrimination may include:

- Refusing to hire an applicant after learning she is pregnant.
- Removing an employee from client-facing work because of pregnancy.
- Denying light duty, schedule changes, seating, breaks, or temporary restrictions.
- Cutting hours after an employee announces a pregnancy.
- Making comments that the employee will be less committed after having a child.
- Pressuring an employee to take leave earlier than medically necessary.
- Firing an employee shortly after she discloses a pregnancy.
- Refusing to reinstate an employee after pregnancy-related leave.
- Retaliating after an employee requests a pregnancy accommodation.
- Treating pregnancy-related medical appointments as attendance problems.
- Firing a pregnant employee for a false reason, such as job restructuring or layoffs.

Pregnancy discrimination may overlap with disability discrimination, leave retaliation, caregiver discrimination, sex discrimination, and hostile work environment claims. For example, an employee who is denied a temporary accommodation for pregnancy-related restrictions may also have rights under disability accommodation laws, depending on the facts. <h2 id="pregnancy-accommodations">What Accommodations Can I Ask For While Pregnant?</h2>

You can ask for reasonable changes at work that let you keep working safely while pregnant, after childbirth, or with a related medical condition. Your employer cannot turn down a reasonable request unless it would cause real hardship to the business, cannot push you onto leave when another change would work, and cannot punish you for asking.

### Common Pregnancy Accommodations

- Extra, longer, or more flexible breaks
- Permission to eat or drink at your workstation
- A stool or chair, a way to work standing, or other changes to equipment or your workstation
- Changes to a uniform or dress code
- Telework
- Light duty, or help with lifting and other physical tasks
- Temporary reassignment, or temporarily pausing one or more job duties
- Time off for prenatal appointments
- Breaks and a private space to pump at work
- Leave to recover from childbirth or a related medical condition

This list isn't complete. Any adjustment that lets you keep doing your job can qualify.

### How to Request a Pregnancy Accommodation

- **Ask HR, your manager, or another supervisor.** Telling a coworker usually isn't enough. You don't need legal terms, and a family member or your doctor can ask for you.
- **Follow up in writing.** Send a short email saying what your limitation is, what you need, and for how long. Keep a copy.
- **Consider a doctor's note.** You don't always need one, but a short note describing your limitation and the change you need can support your request.

Asking for an accommodation is protected, and retaliation for asking is illegal.

### Your Employer Must Discuss Your Request With You

The New York City Human Rights Law requires your employer to hold a good-faith "cooperative dialogue" with you. That means discussing your needs, whether your request is reasonable, and any alternatives, then giving you a written final decision. Skipping that conversation is a separate violation of city law.

<h2 id="leave-prenatal-care-pumping">Your Rights to Leave, Prenatal Care and Pumping at Work</h2>

Pregnancy discrimination doesn't always look like a firing. Sometimes it's a denied appointment, a skipped pumping break, a "restructured" job when you return from leave, or coming back to a performance improvement plan ("PIP").

### Will My Job Be There After Maternity Leave?

If you're covered by the FMLA or New York Paid Family Leave, your employer generally must return you to the same job or one with equivalent pay, benefits, and other terms and conditions. Coming back to a demotion, reduced hours, or a reshuffled role can be illegal retaliation.

In New Jersey, the Family Leave Act gives eligible employees up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave in a 24-month period to bond with a new child. Since July 17, 2026, it covers employers with 15 or more employees. New Jersey Family Leave Insurance can provide partial pay during that time.

### Do I Get Paid Time Off for Prenatal Appointments in New York?

Yes. New York private employers of every size must give you 20 hours of paid prenatal leave in a 52-week period, in addition to your other paid time off, such as sick and vacation leave. You can use it from your first day on the job, in hourly increments, and your employer cannot make you use other paid time off instead.

### Does My Employer Have to Pay Me for Pumping Breaks?

In New York, yes. You're entitled to a paid 30-minute break each time you reasonably need to pump, for up to three years after giving birth, in a private space that is not a bathroom. If you need more than 30 minutes, you can use your other paid breaks or meal time, and you can't be required to stay late to make up the time.

In New Jersey, the Law Against Discrimination requires reasonable break time and a private place to pump. Those breaks don't have to be paid unless they fall within your regular paid breaks.

Your employer cannot punish you for using any of these rights. If it did, [talk to us](/contact).

<h2 id="retaliation">Can My Employer Retaliate for Using Pregnancy Rights?</h2>

No. Your employer cannot punish you for using your pregnancy rights, including requesting a pregnancy accommodation, complaining about pregnancy discrimination, taking protected leave, or opposing discrimination against another pregnant employee. Retaliation may include termination, demotion, reduced hours, negative reviews, loss of responsibilities, hostility, or pressure to resign.

### What Does Pregnancy Retaliation Look Like?

Retaliation is often subtle and is not limited to termination. Common examples include:

- Being put on a performance plan or getting a poor review soon after requesting an accommodation or announcing a pregnancy.
- Losing clients, accounts, or projects "since you'll be out anyway."
- Returning from leave to a smaller role, fewer hours, or a worse schedule.
- Being left out of meetings, travel, or promotion discussions.
- Having breaks or medical appointments closely monitored.
- Hearing that "you don't seem committed anymore."

Suspicious timing can matter. If an employee has strong performance reviews and is suddenly criticized, demoted, or fired shortly after announcing a pregnancy or requesting leave, that timing may support a pregnancy discrimination or retaliation claim.

<h2 id="how-to-prove-pregnancy-discrimination">How Do You Prove Pregnancy Discrimination?</h2>

Most employers never admit pregnancy played a role. Instead, pregnancy discrimination is usually proven through circumstantial evidence: the pattern of what happened before and after your employer learned you were pregnant. Pregnancy doesn't have to be the only reason, and under the NYC Human Rights Law, being treated less well even partly because of pregnancy can be enough.

### What Evidence Proves Pregnancy Discrimination?

- **Timing:** strong reviews, then sudden criticism, a PIP, or a firing after you disclosed a pregnancy, requested an accommodation, or took leave.
- **Review history:** a record of good reviews, raises, or praise before your employer learned you were pregnant.
- **Comments:** remarks about your commitment, your family, or the job being "too much" for you.
- **Comparators:** coworkers who weren't pregnant and were treated better, kept their jobs, or weren't disciplined for the same things.
- **Shifting explanations:** reasons that change over time, or a "restructuring" that only eliminated your role.

For more on building a case after a termination, see [what to do if you were fired while pregnant or on maternity leave](/blog/fired-while-pregnant-or-on-maternity-leave).

### What Should I Save?

Save the requests you put in writing, emails to HR, doctor's notes, text messages, schedule changes, written denials, witness names, and performance reviews from before and after you disclosed your pregnancy. Only keep what you lawfully have access to. A simple timeline of events helps too; bullet points in a Word document are fine.

No. Much of the strongest evidence, like internal emails and how others were treated, is in your employer's hands. We can demand that it be preserved and obtain it in your case. If you sense pregnancy changed how you were treated, [talk to us](/contact).

<h2 id="faq">Pregnancy Discrimination FAQs</h2>

### Do I Have to Tell My Employer I'm Pregnant?

No. You have no legal duty to tell your employer you're pregnant. But your employer only has to accommodate limitations it knows about, so if you need an accommodation or plan to take leave, tell HR or your manager in writing. Written notice also shows what your employer knew, and when.

### Can a Job Interviewer Ask if I'm Pregnant, or Pull an Offer After I Tell Them?

You don't have to answer, and in New York and New Jersey an interviewer generally shouldn't ask. The question itself can be evidence of bias. Job applicants have the same protection as employees, so refusing to hire you or withdrawing a job offer because you're pregnant is illegal discrimination.

### Does My Employer Have to Be a Certain Size?

Federal law, including Title VII and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, covers employers with 15 or more employees. The New York State Human Rights Law and the New Jersey Law Against Discrimination cover employers of any size, and the New York City Human Rights Law covers employers with four or more employees.

### How Long Do I Have to Take Action?

In New York and New Jersey, you generally have 300 days from the last discriminatory act to file a charge with the EEOC. Claims under the New York State and City Human Rights Laws can be filed in court within three years. New Jersey LAD claims must be filed in court within two years, or with the Division on Civil Rights within 180 days. Don't wait. Schedule a consultation early.

### Is the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act Changing? Am I Still Protected?

The law itself isn't changing. The EEOC has announced plans to revise its PWFA regulations, which could narrow which conditions qualify for accommodation. Either way, the New York State and City Human Rights Laws and New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination require pregnancy accommodations on their own, regardless of federal rules.
