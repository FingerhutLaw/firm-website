## Can You Be Laid Off While Pregnant or on Maternity Leave in New York?

Yes, you can be laid off even if you're pregnant. But no, you cannot be laid off, restructured out or fired if your pregnancy played a role in, or motivated, that decision.

Figuring out whether it did can be tricky. It sometimes takes an employment lawyer to unpack the context, the evidence and the documents, and to see whether they support an inference that your pregnancy played a role in ending your employment.

## Why Is "Restructuring" a Common Cover Story for Pregnancy Discrimination?

Because it makes it hard to uncover or prove that pregnancy was the real reason you were laid off, or even a motivating factor.

A restructuring usually comes wrapped in business, operational and financial decisions that look neutral on paper. That's the strategy: bury an unlawful termination underneath facts that make it look like a legitimate, non-discriminatory decision.

Companies think restructuring gives them cover. They'll point to business, cost or operational reasons. It can be daunting to parse through that and uncover what was really going on: that your managers, or others at the company, started seeing you differently once you announced your pregnancy or your plans to take maternity leave.

A false business reason used to cover up the real one is called [pretext](/practice-areas/wrongful-termination).

## 7 Signs Your Layoff Was Pretext for Pregnancy Discrimination

<aside class="checklist" aria-label="Quick checklist">
  <p class="checklist__title">Quick checklist</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Were you the only person laid off?</li>
    <li>Did the layoff come with no notice and as a complete surprise?</li>
    <li>Were your duties reassigned after you announced your pregnancy?</li>
    <li>Was someone hired to replace you, or was your job posted?</li>
    <li>Were the selection criteria never explained, or explained only after you were let go?</li>
    <li>Did the layoff come soon after your announcement, despite good reviews?</li>
    <li>Did the company’s reasons shift, or stay vague?</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### 1. You Were the Only Person Laid Off

If nobody else was laid off or restructured out, just you, that can be a sign the real reason was related to your pregnancy.

### 2. There Was No Notice

Sometimes a restructuring comes up quickly, or the company keeps it quiet. But a lot of times there's chatter, rumors or a feeling around the company that cuts are coming. If yours came as a complete surprise and you're the only one affected, that can be a sign pregnancy played a role.

### 3. Your Duties Were Reassigned After You Announced Your Pregnancy

Look at what happened leading up to the layoff. Were your duties reassigned shortly after you announced you were pregnant, or that you'd be taking maternity leave?

Stripping your duties or unexpectedly changing who you report to is a subtle way companies signal that you're not valued as you once were, or that their perception of you has changed. Expanding your family and putting it first often rubs companies the wrong way. They think you're no longer bought in or a burden.

### 4. Someone Was Hired to Replace You

They tell you your position is being eliminated or restructured. Then, after you're let go, they hire someone who isn't pregnant or planning parental leave, or you see your job posted.

### 5. The Selection Criteria Were Never Explained

Some companies that restructure for legitimate business reasons are transparent about it. They tell you the criteria they're using and who does and doesn't fit them. If your company didn't, or only came up with criteria after you were gone, it may have been trying to bury the real reason it restructured your job.

### 6. The Timing Lines Up With Your Announcement

You're told your job is safe. You get good performance reviews and hear there's upward mobility and a trajectory for you at the company. Then you announce a pregnancy, ask for a pregnancy-related accommodation, or say you plan to take maternity leave. Suddenly the positive feedback is gone, and you've gone from top performer and valued member of the team to a name on the layoff list.

When that shift follows your announcement, the timing may not be a coincidence.

### 7. The Reasons Keep Shifting

This one is very common: the company gives shifting, vague or just plain implausible reasons for letting you go. It keeps moving the target.

People come to me wondering what they did wrong and why they're being let go. I tell them the company is vague on purpose so it can't be pinned down to a specific reason, often because there isn't a legitimate one.

## Companies Have Lawyers and HR. Why Would They Still Get Rid of a Pregnant Employee?

People ask me this all the time: companies have lawyers and HR departments, and haven't we come a long way in supporting pregnant employees? So why would a company still want to get rid of one? A lot of companies do have robust pregnancy protections, but some don't. In my experience, a few things drive it.

### Accommodation Requests and the "Bad Precedent" Fear

Pregnant workers sometimes need [accommodations](/blog/pregnancy-accommodations-under-the-pwfa). Pregnancy is heavy, powerful and sometimes painful to endure, but many pregnant workers want to keep working: they're able, they like their jobs, they're bought in and they find the work rewarding. They just need a simple accommodation to do it.

I've found that some employers resist accommodations because they think it sets a bad precedent or is a burden. Their logic: if you can ask for something while pregnant, other employees will start asking too, morale will suffer and the workforce will look lazier. It also stigmatizes you for needing the accommodation, because people start asking why you're getting it.

So some companies, not all, resist the accommodation conversation, even though New York City law requires a good-faith "cooperative dialogue" when you ask.

### A Change in Perception: "No Longer Bought In"

In the cases I see, pregnancy discrimination usually comes down to stigma: a change in perception.

You're a valued, dedicated employee with ambition. Then you become pregnant, need an accommodation, or take time off (or what your manager views as too much time off, even if the law and company policy allow it). On the ground level, the perception shifts. Management decides your priorities have changed and that your accommodation or leave will burden the team or the manager. I think it comes down to this: they just don't want to deal with it.

### Pushing You Out and Building a Paper Trail

So they decide it's easier to make you uncomfortable enough to leave. They strip responsibilities, micromanage, give you a worse performance review and turn once-positive feedback negative. Each of those sends the same message: we want you to leave.

They hope you'll quit or find a new job. And by papering the micromanagement, the vague criticism and the nitpicking, they think they're building a paper trail to justify firing you if you don't.

### Where the Illegality Lives

It's often in the margins. It's rarely written down, and there usually isn't a smoking gun like "she's pregnant, let's get rid of her." But reading between the lines, the pregnancy is seen as a burden on the company, a burden on the manager and a burden on the team.

## What Documents Can Show Pregnancy Discrimination?

A wide range of documents can support a pregnancy discrimination claim.

### Performance Reviews From Before and After Your Announcement

Performance reviews can show that you were performing well and valued by the company, sometimes for years, before you announced your pregnancy or your need for an accommodation or maternity leave.

Compare that with how your performance was viewed afterward, whether in a review, in emails from your manager or even in verbal comments. The before-and-after can highlight the timing and connect your pregnancy to the shift in how you're perceived.

### Text Messages and Communications With Coworkers

Texts and other messages with coworkers matter too. Coworkers who saw what was happening can corroborate your belief that your pregnancy may have been driving negative decisions about you.

### Management's Explanations for Negative Feedback

Look closely at how management explains the negative feedback over email. Does it check out, or is it exaggerated, distorted or even invented?

If you have years of good performance and suddenly your manager is criticizing things that were never a big deal before, that can help show a causal link between your pregnancy announcement and the negative feedback. That link is what supports a pregnancy discrimination claim.

### Layoff Selection Documents

If the company makes them available, look at the decisional unit, the selection matrix and the list of who was cut. Those can show whether the company followed its own selection criteria, departed from them, or used criteria that were skewed to begin with. If you're 40 or older and were offered severance in a group layoff, federal law (the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act) requires the company to give you the job titles and ages of the people who were and weren't selected, plus at least 45 days to consider the agreement.

## Federal vs. New York and New Jersey Law: Why the "Played Any Role" Standard Matters

Under the federal standard, your pregnancy, accommodation request or maternity leave must have been a motivating factor in the decision to fire you or lay you off.

New York State and New York City set a lower bar: if pregnancy played any role in the decision to fire you, restructure you out or lay you off, that is illegal. New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination asks whether pregnancy was a motivating factor that made a difference in the decision. In a close case, that difference in standards can decide the outcome.

## Offered Severance While Pregnant or on Leave? Don't Sign Yet

If you were offered severance and you think pregnancy discrimination might have played a role, the first thing you should do is contact an employment lawyer.

### Why Companies Want You to Sign Quickly

Severance agreements give companies peace of mind in two ways:

1. **A nondisclosure agreement (NDA).** Most severance agreements include one, which can bar you from talking about what happened: the settlement, the severance pay or your claims.

2. **A general release of claims.** You give up the right to sue the company for anything that happened while you worked there.

With those two terms in place, the company doesn't have to worry about you talking or coming back in a year to sue. That's why it wants you to sign quickly, before you talk to a lawyer.

If you think pregnancy played a role, have an employment lawyer review the agreement before you sign. See also: [Should You Sign That Severance Agreement? Five Red Flags](/blog/should-you-sign-that-severance-agreement).

### Can an NDA Really Stop You From Talking About It in New York?

Yes, if you agree to it. In New York, an agreement resolving a discrimination claim can keep the underlying facts confidential only if confidentiality is your preference, and it can't make you forfeit your severance or pay liquidated damages (e.g., a set amount of money as a penalty for breaching) if you break it. But a valid NDA is still a contract, and if the company can prove you breached it, you could be sued or the deal can fall through. That's why I usually recommend saying as little as possible about what happened while you worked there.

There are limits. You can usually still talk to your spouse (and sometimes immediate family), your lawyer, a tax professional and a mental health professional, and you can still report to government agencies and law enforcement. You also get up to 21 days to decide whether you want confidentiality, and 7 more days after signing to change your mind.

### The Deadline to Sign Is Often Negotiable

Companies will also give you a deadline to sign. Depending on your age and the terms of the deal, the law may guarantee you a minimum amount of time to consider it, such as 21 days. But the company’s own deadline is often arbitrary and can usually be extended beyond whatever minimum the law gives you.

You don't want to ignore the company entirely. But the company's preferred deadline isn't a legal requirement. If you need time to find a lawyer or think it over, or your lawyer needs time to strategize, those deadlines are routinely extended without the money being pulled off the table.

## What to Do This Week If You Were Laid Off While Pregnant or on Maternity Leave

1. **Gather the documents and evidence you have access to.** That includes emails, performance reviews and messages with coworkers, or even people outside work you were confiding in while this was happening. Keep them handy; they can be hard to dig up later.

2. **Be careful how you save emails.** A lot of companies have software that alerts them when you forward an email from your work account to a personal one. If you don't want them to know you're gathering documents, take a picture of the email on your screen with your phone instead, so there's no record of you forwarding it. **Only save what you lawfully have access to, and don't take confidential company files.**

3. **Type up a timeline of events.** Bullet points in a Word document are fine; just write it on a personal device, not a work computer. Writing it down often jogs memories that don't come up when you're just talking it through, and it puts the whole story on paper for a lawyer to review. A detail you didn't think mattered may be exactly what a lawyer spots as leverage or helpful evidence.

## FAQs: Layoffs, Pregnancy and Maternity Leave in New York

### Can I Be Laid Off While on FMLA Leave?

Yes, you can be laid off while on FMLA leave, but only for legitimate reasons that aren't retaliatory or discriminatory. If the reason, or one of the reasons, is that you took the leave, that's illegal retaliation or interference with your FMLA rights. Learn more about [FMLA retaliation and interference](/practice-areas/fmla-retaliation).

### Do I Get My Job Back if the Company Rehires?

Possibly. Reinstatement can be a term of a settlement, although it's often not on the table. And I normally caution people to think long and hard before going back to a place that fired them for discriminatory reasons in the first place. It may not be the best place to work.

### What if They Offered Me Another Role at Lower Pay?

If you're on protected leave, the company is supposed to hold your job, or an equivalent one, for when you come back. It doesn't always have to be the exact job you had, but it should be substantially similar, including in responsibilities and pay. Bringing you back to what's essentially a demotion is illegal.

### What if My Employer Says It Was Just a Layoff?

In my experience, if your instincts tell you it wasn't just a layoff and may be related to your pregnancy, trust them. Have an employment lawyer look at the context, the evidence and the timing to figure out whether it was a legitimate termination or whether pregnancy played a role.
